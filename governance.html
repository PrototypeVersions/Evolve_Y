A platform can distribute its hardware while keeping all meaningful power at the center. The proposed design distributes rights, oversight and choice—and assigns clear responsibility for operations.

Proposed social contract

Six promises that
need teeth.

These principles are a recommendation for the future platform. They have not been adopted as amendments to any existing company’s charter.

01

Equal dignity

Access and treatment must not depend on ancestry, religion, nationality, disability, wealth or political conformity. Protect people of every faith and no faith. Apply lawful, specific eligibility requirements only where the service requires them.

02

Agency & exit

People can decline optional features, revoke future permissions and leave with usable copies of records they are entitled to possess. Explain legal retention duties and shared-record limits before participation.

03

Clear economic rights

Publish fees, eligibility, allocation formulas and conflicts. No hidden ranking payments. Member-reserved rights and distribution rules belong in enforceable documents, not just website copy.

04

Privacy by default

Collect what the task needs. Separate identity verification from a person’s general profile. Offer non-biometric alternatives. No public personal-data ledger or universal behavioral score.

05

Reasons & recourse

Explain consequential decisions, identify the responsible party and provide timely human review. Preserve evidence and make corrections visible without exposing private information.

06

A right to be included

Design for disability, low bandwidth, different languages and limited resources. Budget for access. Contribution-based rewards cannot be the only route to benefit.

Who decides?

Different decisions.
Different authority.

Operational speed and democratic legitimacy need not be enemies. Give people a say where their rights are at stake, and give operators responsibility for delivering the service.

A service failure

The operator acts immediately.

The service team restores availability, contains incidents and notifies affected people. A named incident owner publishes a review. Members can appeal the handling; an emergency is not permission to rewrite ownership rules.

Checks on power

Separate the roles
that should check one another.

A single group should not sell the service, hold the money, certify its own fairness and decide every dispute without independent challenge.

Proposed roleResponsibilityConstraint
Operating companyProduct development, employment, delivery, billing and incident response.Transparent accounts, contracts, defined service obligations and external review.
Member councilRepresent users and contributors; approve reserved changes to fees and benefit rules.Verified membership, fair elections, term limits and published conflicts. No token-weighted purchase of civic voice.
Independent rights stewardReview rights violations, conflicts and proposed changes to the charter.Independent selection and budget, reasoned decisions, rotation and a removal process for misconduct.
Specialist providersPayments, legal services, regulated financial activity and independent assurance.Separate responsibilities, suitable qualifications and documented agreements. A partner logo is not evidence.
Local operatorsDeliver context-sensitive services, support members and build local demand.Common quality and rights standards; no exclusive territorial monopoly or forced vendor lock-in.

A credible transition from founder control

Founders need room to build. Before paid launch, propose a signed rights charter. By a defined membership milestone, elect user and worker representatives. Reserve changes to core rights for a supermajority plus independent review. Publish the actual cap table and voting map when an ownership program is considered.

Prevent governance from becoming theater

Fund oversight; disclose related-party transactions; publish decision records; protect complaints; and conduct a real export-and-switch exercise. Members must be able to challenge the operator without losing ordinary access. A constitution that no one can enforce is only a statement of intent.

Human authority in an AI-rich world

Capable assistance.
Bounded permission.

  • Approve consequential action.AI can draft, compare and propose. Contracts, payments and other significant commitments require the appropriate person’s authorization.
  • Keep permissions outside the model.Enforce tool access, spending limits and data boundaries in the application. A prompt alone is not an access control.
  • Evaluate behavior, not self-description.Test accuracy, leakage, unsafe action attempts, failure recovery and disparate impacts. A fluent explanation is not evidence of faithful internal reasoning.
  • Preserve the ability to stop.Use short-lived credentials, logs, staged deployment, rollback and incident response. Model changes do not automatically expand authority.
  • Learn without taking people’s data.Use opted-in or suitably de-identified evaluation data with documented limitations. No silent training on private projects.
Centralization vs decentralization

Choose the structure
for the task.

Centralize responsibility for security, reliability and support at the start. Distribute delivery, data rights, representation and ownership where enforceable arrangements make that meaningful. Make providers replaceable and interfaces open.

Federation adds real costs: version compatibility, assurance, disputes, identity coordination and weaker network-wide control. Introduce it only after the core workflow is stable. The aim is resilience and freedom, not decentralization as a slogan.

The platform remains a private service within society. It does not assume the authority of governments, courts, central banks, religious institutions or the people it serves.

Trust should survive a change of leadership.

Build the charter, technical controls and economics together. Then test them in the real world.
