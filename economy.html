The initial product is a paid service for organizations. Customer acquisition, human review and support are real costs; AI does not make them disappear.

Why it could be profitable

A repeatable workflow and reusable integrations can reduce delivery cost across many customers. Local support, trusted records and predictable execution create value beyond access to a generic AI model.

Why it might fail

Customers may prefer existing software. Human review can consume the margin. Sales may be expensive. AI improvements may make the service a commodity. A small paid pilot tests these possibilities before infrastructure spending.

The calculation is intentionally visible.

Revenue = paying organizations × price. Direct cost = organizations × direct cost per organization. Operating profit = revenue − direct cost − fixed cost. Positive operating profit funds the tax provision; positive after-tax profit funds the reserve. The remaining positive amount is distributable surplus.

The chosen sharing percentage creates a common pool: 65% for eligible members and 35% for community access. The remainder stays with the operating company. The equal member division is a simple illustration; final eligibility and allocation require an agreed charter. Amounts are rounded for display; no extra money is created by rounding.

This one-month model excludes a detailed balance sheet, working-capital timing, financing, depreciation and capital purchases. Enter an appropriate allowance in costs or reserves before using it for planning. It does not model churn, customer acquisition over time, tax law or the additional staff required at scale. A positive result is not proof of commercial feasibility.

At 20 customers using the default price and cost assumptions, the pilot loses $8,800 per month before startup costs. That learning period needs an explicit budget. New customers’ capital must never be disguised as earned surplus.
Four distinct benefits

Share the gains.
Do not count them twice.

Customer savings, wages, ownership and company distributions are different things. The proposed network must make each one visible.

01

Useful savings

Customers keep reduced costs and time. A saved hour is not automatically cash income, and does not by itself create a dividend.

02

Fair payment

Workers and providers are paid for delivered work under clear contracts. Their ordinary pay is a cost, not a discretionary community benefit.

03

Shared surplus

A published, enforceable rule allocates a portion of residual earnings after costs, taxes and reserves. Payouts can be zero.

04

Wider access

A separate community budget supports people who cannot purchase or contribute. The proposed allocation is selected with member oversight.

Proposed ownership is a legal and governance question. A profile, token, device purchase or membership label does not establish equity, a guaranteed payout or voting rights. Any investment arrangement remains separate, with its own proper documentation and review.
The bigger economic question

What if AI makes
high income universal?

It would be a profound opportunity. But technical abundance does not decide who owns productive assets, who receives purchasing power or how much housing, energy, care and land are available. Those are economic and political questions.

A credible long-term vision combines three routes: make essential goods and services more abundant; broaden claims on productive value; and support institutions that provide income security. Public transfers, social insurance, public services, worker ownership and shorter working time can play different roles. This company cannot enact or finance that settlement by itself.

If jobs remain important, the network should make people more capable and improve work. If paid labor becomes less central, it should help people organize care, education, creativity and community life without making human worth depend on employment.

Measure freedom as well as income.

Track essential costs, time autonomy, access, meaningful choice and who is left out. A larger payout alongside rising housing costs or lost rights may not be progress.

A necessary correction

Purchases do not
pay for themselves.

The original sites sometimes propose reimbursing purchases using a premium directed into productive infrastructure. That ambition needs a different funding mechanism.

The funding-base problem

In the published example, a customer pays $1,340 for $1,000 of goods and is described as receiving $1,340 after three years. If $1,000 pays the merchant, only $340 remains to fund the future payment. Without another funding source, $340 must grow to $1,340: about 58% per year before costs.

Calculation: (1,340 ÷ 340)1/3 − 1 ≈ 57.96%. This tests the stated example’s funding arithmetic, not the performance of an audited operating business. Original source ↗

A workable replacement

Offer an immediately useful service at a disclosed price. Pay modest rebates only from verified net earnings or a separately identified, fully funded promotional budget. Never promise full purchase reimbursement based on assumed returns.

Do not call revenue surplus, confuse token valuation with liquid money, or impose the same revenue split on every industry. Financing, settlement, taxation, insurance and monetary policy have different functions.

A profitable business. A visible public benefit.

Both should stand up to scrutiny. The next question is who can hold the system to its promises.
